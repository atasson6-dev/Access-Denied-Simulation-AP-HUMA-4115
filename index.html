Behind the experience

What barriers does the research identify?

Virtual care can be difficult to access

Canadian patients with chronic conditions described barriers involving technology access, internet reliability, digital skills, and technical problems. These findings inform Jordan’s limited connectivity and Margaret’s difficulty navigating digital systems.

Chan-Nguyen et al. (2022) · Canadian study ↗

Online appointment booking does not work equally for everyone

A study of primary-care appointment booking in England examined patient use and experiences. It found differences in engagement with online booking across patient groups, including by age and socioeconomic circumstances, underscoring the value of accessible alternatives.

Atherton et al. (2024) · England study ↗

Who makes decisions about healthcare technology?

A qualitative study at a Canadian academic centre examined how clinicians, managers, patients, researchers, and technology providers experience the introduction of AI in healthcare. Their priorities can differ, raising questions about oversight and real-world usability.

Alami et al. (2024) · Canadian study ↗

AI-assisted scheduling can reinforce racial inequities

Shanklin and colleagues examine how appointment-scheduling algorithms using historical attendance data can produce longer waits for Black patients in the United States. This is a separate, brief example of how automated scheduling can reinforce inequity; Jordan’s and Margaret’s fictional booking experiences do not assume AI is involved.

Shanklin et al. (2022) · United States ↗
Beyond the screen

Who is responsible for these barriers?

Clinics and healthcare organizations

Select and evaluate booking platforms, maintain phone and in-person alternatives, and respond to unsuccessful booking attempts.

Platform designers and vendors

Build accessible interfaces, minimize unnecessary mandatory fields, offer flexible confirmation methods, and test with diverse users.

Clinicians and frontline staff

Recognize when digital systems delay access, assist patients through alternative channels, and raise recurring issues with decision-makers.

Health system leaders

Set accessibility expectations, evaluate who is excluded, and ensure accountability when digital services become barriers to care.

Core takeaway

Making appointments available online does not guarantee that everyone can book one. Equitable access requires accessible design, flexibility, and meaningful human alternatives.

Health and medical research only
