What the Research Found

Shanklin et al. (2022) · Who Waits Longer?

Philosophy professor Robert Shanklin and colleagues’ 2022 article, “Ethical Redress of Racial Inequities in AI: Lessons from Decoupling Machine Learning from Optimization in Medical Appointment Scheduling,” argues that accurate predictions can still produce unfair appointment schedules. The authors explain how algorithms which are trained on previous data from the past can affect black patients by making them wait longer to book appointments, reproducing existing healthcare inequalities. They distinguish between machine learning, which predicts attendance, and optimization, which uses those predictions to schedule appointments. Drawing on Samorani and colleagues’ scheduling experiments, Section 5 presents a comparison of four approaches, which are removing machine learning, excluding socioeconomic information, and changing scheduling objectives either with or without explicitly considering race. The race-aware algorithm did not create a significant racial disparity, and schedule quality was only 1% worse than the existing method. Removing just the socioeconomic data did not remove the disparities (though it was reduced, it remained at 10%) as other data including attendance history remained correlated with race. The authors conclude that changing how schedules are optimized can reduce unfairness while preserving much of the system’s efficiency.

Read Shanklin et al. on Springer Nature

Atherton et al. (2024) · Online Booking in England

Primary care research professor Helen Atherton and colleagues’ 2024 article, “Investigating Patient Use and Experience of Online Appointment Booking in Primary Care: Mixed Methods Study,” examines who uses online appointment booking and how patients experience it. The researchers analyzed 1,327,693 survey responses from 2018–2019 and interviewed 43 patients across 11 general practices. Their results show that approximately 45% of respondents answering the awareness question knew online booking was available, while roughly 16% answering the usage question had used it. Awareness and use declined sharply with age (>75 years) and patients in poorer areas were overrepresented in practices with lower levels of awareness and use. People with long-term conditions were more likely to use the internet to make appointments, particularly for routine and non-urgent appointments. Interviewees mentioned convenience as a benefit, while registration procedures and limited appointment availability impacted their experiences. Older interviewees often preferred to book by telephone. The authors conclude that patient circumstances and practice arrangements influence engagement, and recommend supporting patients who want to book online, while maintaining telephone access for those who cannot or prefer not to.

Read Atherton et al. on the journal website · PubMed record

Alturkistani et al. (2023) · Who Uses Online Booking?

Primary care and public health researcher Abrar Alturkistani and colleagues’ 2023 article, “Cross-sectional analyses of online appointment booking and repeat prescription ordering user characteristics in general practices of England in the years 2018–2020,” examines how patient characteristics are associated with online service use. Using 1,807,049 survey responses, the researchers conducted statistical analyses accounting for differences between general practices. Approximately 15% of the respondents had booked appointments online, and 19% had ordered repeat prescriptions online during the previous year. Respondents who were 85 or older were less likely to use either of the services than younger people. Black respondents were less likely than White respondents to use either service. Respondents from the least deprived areas were more likely than respondents from the most deprived areas to use both services. Chronic conditions, the taking of five or more regular medications, and deafness or hearing loss were associated with higher odds of use. The authors recognize that self-reported usage and exclusion of incomplete responses may bias the results. They conclude that alternatives should be offered for patients unable or unwilling to use online services and that further research is needed to distinguish barriers from personal preferences.

Read Alturkistani et al. on ProQuest · Open-access journal version

Relevance to Canadian Healthcare

Although these studies examine healthcare systems in the United States and England, their findings highlight barriers that are also relevant to Canadian healthcare. Our simulation applies these international findings to explore how digital appointment booking may reinforce existing inequalities and create additional barriers to accessing healthcare in Canada.

Beyond the Screen

Who Is Responsible for These Barriers?

Who Are the People Responsible Behind These AI Booking Tools?

Software developers, AI engineers, data scientists, platform designers, healthcare administrators and health system leaders are responsible for developing, implementing and overseeing AI booking tools. They must ensure these systems are accessible, equitable and do not reinforce existing healthcare inequalities.

Clinics and Healthcare Organizations

Select and evaluate booking platforms, maintain phone and in-person alternatives, and respond to unsuccessful booking attempts.

Platform Designers and Vendors

Build accessible interfaces, minimize unnecessary mandatory fields, offer flexible confirmation methods, and test with diverse users.

Clinicians and Frontline Staff

Recognize when digital systems delay access, assist patients through alternative channels, and raise recurring issues with decision-makers.

Health System Leaders

Set accessibility expectations, evaluate who is excluded, and ensure accountability when digital services become barriers to care.

How Do Doctors Look at This?

Doctors may recognize the convenience of online appointment booking while acknowledging that digital barriers can delay necessary healthcare. Our research emphasizes the importance of human assistance, telephone booking and equitable scheduling.

Input from Kinesiology and Health Science Professors — York University, Faculty of Health

Our discussions with two professors highlighted the connection between our project and Kinesiology and Health Science, particularly healthcare accessibility, health equity and the social determinants of health. Feedback emphasized how barriers between patients and digital platforms can prevent marginalized individuals from receiving necessary care in Canada.

Core Takeaway

Making appointments available online does not guarantee that everyone can book one. Equitable access requires accessible design, flexibility, and meaningful human alternatives.
